# Heata pays the power bill; DigitalHeat should too

Heata, a UK company registered as Bit Warmer Limited, runs on one simple deal. It owns a refurbished ~330 W server that clamps onto a household's vented hot-water cylinder, **pays the server's electricity by crediting the host's energy or bank account from a billing submeter**, and gives the household the heat for free. It earns money by selling batch cloud compute to businesses. Nine years in, the model works technically but not commercially. The last hard fleet count was **80 units installed (Aug 2023)**. The company had **7 staff, £117k cash and roughly £375k a year of losses at end-2025**, and trade debtors were just £340, which points to very little paying compute. It is still active in Nov 2026, kept going by small angel top-ups, ~£0.79m of UK grants and a fresh government pilot win. The comparable operators tell the same story. Every one that puts servers in someone else's building pays the electricity. The residential pioneers failed or pivoted (Nerdalize bankrupt, Cloud&Heat's home sites closed, Qarnot insolvent and sold to Scaleway in 2026), and only larger, building-scale hosts such as Leafcloud survive. For DigitalHeat the answer to "are you sure DigitalHeat pays for the electricity?" is this. The case brief does not say, but Heata and every comparator do, so the defensible default is **operator pays electricity, measured on a submeter**, with the heat price as an explicit lever. The deeper lesson is that the deal only moves money between house and company. On the reference house the whole chain is **255 CHF/yr short** at the central compute price, so compute price, sold share and hardware cost decide viability whichever way the electricity bill is assigned.

*How to read this report: sentences with a linked source are sourced facts. Sentences or paragraphs marked **Inference** are our own reasoning or arithmetic from those facts. DigitalHeat figures marked [model] come from the project's [one_rack_model.py](../analysis/one_rack_model.py) on the case's reference house (Basel 2025, 0.3325 CHF/kWh, heat-pump COP 2.5).*

## A 330 W server on a vented tank, with the power bill refunded

**The hardware is small and deliberately simple.** Each heata unit is a re-configured dual-Xeon server ("56 cores in total") with no GPU in any home unit we found ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2023/01/12/heata_offers_free_hot_water/)). It bolts onto a patented thermal bridge fixed to the outside of a **vented** copper cylinder after a patch of foam insulation is cut away, so heat conducts through the cylinder wall. There is no plumbing, the unit is wired into the immersion-heater circuit, and the existing immersion heater or boiler does the top-up ([Feb 2022 product sheet](https://uploads-ssl.webflow.com/6172c719d273286e95006270/6230cfe91838b1e5d763cab5_heata-compute%20powered%20water%20heater_Feb22.pdf)). The current site rates it at **"330W compute, including 220W harnessed from waste heat"** ([heata unit page](https://www.heata.co/free-hot-water/heata-unit)). The CTO said units put "between 180W and 200W into the tank when at full power", which is at most 4.8 kWh/day ([Register forum](https://forums.theregister.com/forum/all/2023/01/12/heata_offers_free_hot_water/)). A version for unvented cylinders is still "in development" ([heata unit page](https://www.heata.co/free-hot-water/heata-unit)). When the water is at temperature, the processors throttle and the unit "won't be able to run". It never dumps heat elsewhere ([heata FAQ](https://www.heata.co/free-hot-water)). Heata expects hardware to become "outdated / unprofitable to run (perhaps 4 years)" before it fails ([Feb 2022 product sheet](https://uploads-ssl.webflow.com/6172c719d273286e95006270/6230cfe91838b1e5d763cab5_heata-compute%20powered%20water%20heater_Feb22.pdf)).

**Heata pays the electricity, and the mechanism is a submeter credit, not a separate supply.** The live FAQ reads: "heata pays for the electricity used by the heata unit … we'll either directly credit your energy account, or your bank account. An accredited billing submeter with a readable screen is included as part of the installation, which we will read remotely" ([heata FAQ](https://www.heata.co/free-hot-water)). In 2022 the credit was promised "at 10% above the market rate" ([Feb 2022 product sheet](https://uploads-ssl.webflow.com/6172c719d273286e95006270/6230cfe91838b1e5d763cab5_heata-compute%20powered%20water%20heater_Feb22.pdf)). The British Gas trial release repeats that "heata pays for the electricity the heata unit uses" ([Centrica, Feb 2025](https://www.centrica.com/media-centre/news/2025/british-gas-partners-with-heata-on-trial-to-reuse-waste-heat-from-data-processing/)).

**The household gets free hot-water baseload and nothing else.** Heata models the saving on "hot water heated with electricity at 22p/kWh, or heated with gas at 7.8p/kWh … with the heata unit supplying 4.25kWh of hot water per day" ([heata unit page](https://www.heata.co/free-hot-water/heata-unit)). It advertises **"Save £150–350 a year"** ([heata FAQ](https://www.heata.co/free-hot-water)), or "up to £120" when the offset fuel is gas ([Centrica, Feb 2025](https://www.centrica.com/media-centre/news/2025/british-gas-partners-with-heata-on-trial-to-reuse-waste-heat-from-data-processing/)). Trial units and installs were free because it was "a government-backed trial" ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2023/01/12/heata_offers_free_hot_water/)), and hosts cannot use the compute themselves ([Feb 2022 product sheet](https://uploads-ssl.webflow.com/6172c719d273286e95006270/6230cfe91838b1e5d763cab5_heata-compute%20powered%20water%20heater_Feb22.pdf)). Installation takes an electrician "under two hours" (2022) or "within a day" (now). British Gas tested the procedure, and a cylinder maker confirmed it does not void the warranty ([heata FAQ](https://www.heata.co/free-hot-water)). No consumer price, host contract length or post-trial terms have ever been published.

**The trials were small and have no published results.** The Innovate UK project behind the pilot planned to "build 100 alpha prototypes and run a 12-month pilot in 100 homes" ([GtR 10017625](https://gtr.ukri.org/find-research-and-innovation/project/eb7681e0-9ddc-4561-a925-1bad02531fa6)). MIT Technology Review reported **80 units installed in Surrey by Aug 2023, with 30 more planned** ([MIT TR](https://www.technologyreview.com/2023/08/18/1077548/computer-waste-heat/)). British Gas then ran a **10-home, three-month employee trial** from Feb 2025 on its own workloads ([Centrica](https://www.centrica.com/media-centre/news/2025/british-gas-partners-with-heata-on-trial-to-reuse-waste-heat-from-data-processing/)). We found no independent evaluation, fleet utilisation figure or published British Gas outcome. The only measured output in public is one host's anecdote of "over ten tonnes of hot water" ([heata, Dec 2023](https://www.heata.co/content-hub/did-you-spot-us-on-bbc-news)). Criticism comes from tech forums rather than hosts: the small heat output, electricity as an expensive heat source, the tank filling up in summer, doubts about heat transfer through the cylinder wall, short hardware life and truck-roll costs ([Register forum](https://forums.theregister.com/forum/all/2023/01/12/heata_offers_free_hot_water/)).

**Inference.** About two thirds of each kWh Heata buys reaches the water (220/330 W). The rest warms the airing cupboard. Because output is capped by demand at ~4.7 kWh/day of hot water, utilisation, and therefore compute revenue, falls in summer and in low-use homes.

## Compute sales decide Heata's fate, and sales are thin

**Heata sells interruptible batch compute, backed by public cloud.** It started with 3D rendering for studios such as F10, Eleven Visualisation and Model Works/Landsec ([heata render page](https://www.heata.co/render/mediacymru)). It now offers Kubernetes, disposable VM clusters, GitHub Actions runners, Ray via Anyscale and Kubeflow, pitched at "stateless, parallel workloads" ([heata compute](https://www.heata.co/compute)). The only enterprise customer it names is **Centrica**, and the "trusted by" strip shows Centrica and DEFRA ([heata compute](https://www.heata.co/compute)). Customers connect through always-on access nodes "hosted within GCP", while in-home workers are ephemeral ([docs](https://docs.heata.co/)). The August 2026 SLA says Heata "may use cloud instances (Cloud Burst Nodes) in addition to our distributed Worker Nodes" to guarantee delivery. It also reserves the right to run its own work on a client's idle allocated capacity "to … ensure reasonable hot water delivery to the household(s)" ([Standard SLA 2026](https://docs.heata.co/downloads/heata%20_Standard%20SLA_2026.pdf)). An earlier design constraint required each node to keep ">50%" monthly utilisation because it heats a home ([heata blog, Feb 2024](https://www.heata.co/content-hub/data-centre-carbon-hackathon-microsoft-azure-centralised-vs-heata-distributed)). The SLA promises >99.5% committed capacity, and spot capacity "carries no SLA" ([heata compute](https://www.heata.co/compute)).

**List prices are public and low.** Managed compute costs **£0.017 per vCPU-hour** and spot **£0.010**. RTX 4090-class GPUs add **£0.89 per GPU-hour**, and reserved terms take 30% (1 yr) or 55% (3 yr) off ([pricing docs](https://docs.heata.co/pricing-billing/)). A Wales render farm, which heats a community building rather than homes, charges £2 per GPU-hour ([heata render page](https://www.heata.co/render/mediacymru)). In 2024 Heata priced by node instead: £0.75 per node-hour pay-as-you-go, down to a £210 per node per month "Heavy" plan ([Wayback, Jun 2024](https://web.archive.org/web/20240623112054/https://www.heata.co/compute)). Its marketing claim is "up to 50% cheaper than the hyperscalers", and no worked comparison is published ([heata compute](https://www.heata.co/compute)).

**Funding has been small and partly public.** UKRI records **£791,590 in grants** to Bit Warmer over 2020–2026 ([GtR](https://gtr.ukri.org/organisation/45FE7A8F-A252-4E52-BBBD-C774B4E8CA93)). The headline equity round was a **£1m seed in Sep 2023** led by Civo CEO Mark Boost with Green Angel ([heata seed post](https://www.heata.co/content-hub/announcement-weve-just-secured-1-million-in-funding)). Share premium reached £2.12m by end-2025, and a further 10,335 shares were issued at £21.92 in June 2026, about £227k ([Companies House filings](https://find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk/company/11110428/filing-history)). British Gas Trading Limited owns about 8.2%, and Heata began in 2017 as a British Gas fuel-poverty innovation project ([seed post](https://www.heata.co/content-hub/announcement-weve-just-secured-1-million-in-funding); [Companies House filings](https://find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk/company/11110428/filing-history)).

**Status in Nov 2026: alive, tiny, and repositioning toward government compute.** Companies House lists the company as active with no charges ([overview](https://find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk/company/11110428)). At 31 Dec 2025 it held £116,647 cash, £96,077 net assets and £2.18m accumulated losses, with 7 average employees. It files small-company accounts, so turnover is not disclosed ([filings](https://find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk/company/11110428/filing-history)). On 9 Oct 2026 it won "Pitch in Whitehall: Green Compute", which "comes with a pilot contract with government" ([heata blog](https://www.heata.co/content-hub/heata-wins-pitch-in-whitehall-green-compute)). The jobs page lists no openings ([heata jobs](https://www.heata.co/jobs)), and households can only register interest "for a future pilot" ([hosts sign-up](https://www.heata.co/hosts-sign-up)).

**Inference.** Year-end trade debtors of £340 (2025) and £6,718 (2024), together with no capitalised server fleet (fixed assets £1,989), suggest commercial revenue in the low tens of thousands of pounds at most. The fleet has probably stayed around 100 homes since 2023. Changes in accumulated losses imply a burn of about £375k a year, which leaves only months of runway after the June 2026 raise. The pattern is a company with a cheap, working heat side and too few compute buyers. Electricity is its dominant variable cost: about £510–580 per unit per year at its own 80%-utilisation assumption and 22–25p/kWh. That cost is only covered if units are actually sold. Grants supplied roughly a quarter of all outside money (£0.79m of about £2.9m) and paid for the 100-prototype pilot.

**The failures share an economic cause, not a technical one.** Cloud&Heat called its approach "technologically convincing" while "the business model … ultimately proved unsuccessful" ([Cloud&Heat](https://www.cloudandheat.com/en/news-press/once-upon-a-time-there-was-a-cloud-that-heated-homes-worldwide/)). Qarnot reached only **€3m revenue in 2024** with €9m of losses over 2023–24, and €35m raised "did not balance the accounts" ([Next.ink](https://next.ink/247072/scaleway-rachete-qarnot-et-ses-activites-hpc-recemment-placees-en-redressement-judiciaire)). Scaleway bought it to strengthen its cloud and AI platform with low-carbon HPC, so the buyer saw value in the compute, not the heat ([Scaleway](https://www.scaleway.com/en/news/scaleway-acquires-qarnot-to-strengthen-its-european-cloud-and-ai-platform-with-low-carbon-hpc-technology/)). Leafcloud, the closest live match to DigitalHeat, sizes sites at 5–20% of a building's design thermal load. It requires at least 500 GJ/yr of hot-water demand, three-phase power and existing hot-water infrastructure, and delivers 45–65 °C ([Leafcloud](https://www.leaf.cloud/leafsites/)).

**Inference.** "Operator pays electricity, host gets heat free" is the market standard a Swiss host will expect. Deals that asked hosts for money (AoTerra, Nerdalize) belonged to companies that later exited, though that is correlation, not proof. Survivors moved to larger units, which suggests per-site overhead (install, connectivity, service visits) dominates below a few kW. DigitalHeat's 5–20 kW racks sit at the bottom of Leafcloud's band, which is where the evidence says the model can live.

## The deal only moves money; the chain stays 255 CHF short

**The case does not assign the electricity bill.** The brief fixes one flat electricity price and values server heat as the heat-pump electricity it replaces (price ÷ 2.5). It never says who pays the server's power ([case brief](../docs/case-brief.md)). Its break-even metric, `(electricity cost − value of the heat actually used) ÷ server-hours sold`, is a whole-chain number that does not depend on the split. On the reference house it comes to **0.283 CHF per sold hour** before hardware [model]. The three deals below are therefore three ways of dividing the same year: 7,629 server-hours, 2,537 CHF of electricity and 6,103 kWh of used heat worth 812 CHF [model].

| Deal | Electricity | Heat price to house | House vs today | DigitalHeat P&L (marketplace, 80% kept) | DigitalHeat P&L (sold direct) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A (Heata's deal) | DigitalHeat | Free | **+812 CHF** | **−1,778 CHF** | −1,067 CHF |
| Split (our proposal) | DigitalHeat | 0.067 CHF/kWh (half of heat-pump equivalent) | +406 CHF | −1,372 CHF | −661 CHF |
| B | DigitalHeat | 0.133 CHF/kWh (heat-pump equivalent) | 0 CHF | −966 CHF | −255 CHF |
| C | House | Free | **−1,725 CHF** | +759 CHF | — |

*All rows [model] at 0.582 CHF per server-hour, 80% of run hours sold, 8,924 CHF hardware and install over 5 years, 300 CHF/yr opex. The Split row and the direct-sale column are extra runs of the same script.*

**Recommendation: model the Heata structure, with DigitalHeat paying the server electricity through a billing submeter, and make the heat price an explicit parameter.** Report Deal A as the headline for the reference house. It is what Heata, Qarnot's housing deployments, Deep Green and Leafcloud all offer, so it is the deal a homeowner or a jury will benchmark against, and it answers the "who pays" question with real-world evidence rather than an assumption. Show B as the ceiling of what heat can contribute to DigitalHeat's revenue, and propose the Split as the opening offer for a real pilot. **Drop C to a one-line sensitivity.** It leaves the household 1,725 CHF a year worse off. No comparator that places servers in someone else's building asks that, and the only cases where the host pays the power are consumer crypto heaters, which are hardware sales.

**Inference: why a heat fee, even a small one, matters operationally.** Running one extra server-hour costs 0.3325 CHF of electricity and delivers 0.8 kWh of heat worth 0.106 CHF to the house. The chain's marginal cost of an hour is therefore 0.226 CHF. Under Deal A the house keeps the heat value, so DigitalHeat should refuse any job paying less than 0.3325 CHF per server-hour, even when the chain as a whole would gain. Low-price filler work, such as the 0.269 CHF Salad batch tier in the model, then never runs, the tank cools and the house loses heat it would have had. A heat fee hands part of the heat value back to the party deciding whether to run the job. That aligns incentives the way Heata's SLA right to run filler jobs does. At half the heat-pump equivalent, DigitalHeat's threshold falls to about 0.279 CHF, and at B to 0.226 CHF.

**The decisive point is that no split closes the gap.** The whole chain must cover 3,810 CHF a year (electricity minus avoided heat-pump power, plus hardware and opex), while the client pays 3,555 CHF. That is **255 CHF short before any platform fee**, and the chain only breaks even at 0.624 CHF per sold server-hour (0.372 USD per GPU-hour) [model]. If the hardware lasts Heata's "perhaps 4 years" instead of the model's 5, the chain deficit grows to **701 CHF/yr** and Deal A's loss to 2,224 CHF [model run with 4-year life]. The levers that move the result are price, sold share, selling direct rather than through a marketplace, and capex per kWh of heat, not who signs the electricity contract.

## Seven Heata habits worth copying, three that do not travel to Basel

**Sell deadlines and back them with a fallback.** Heata guarantees throughput rather than particular machines, and covers gaps with Cloud Burst Nodes it records and discloses ([Standard SLA 2026](https://docs.heata.co/downloads/heata%20_Standard%20SLA_2026.pdf)). *Inference:* DigitalHeat's scheduler already tracks missed deadlines (a case metric), so a priced burst fallback turns each miss into a cost line instead of a broken promise. Because DigitalHeat's pitch is "Switzerland local data and operations" ([case presentation](../docs/case-presentation.md)), that fallback has to be Swiss-hosted, not a hyperscaler region, or the sovereignty claim falls apart.

**Keep a filler queue to keep tanks warm.** Heata reserves the right to run its own sandboxed work on a client's idle allocated capacity to "ensure reasonable hot water delivery" ([SLA](https://docs.heata.co/downloads/heata%20_Standard%20SLA_2026.pdf)). *Inference:* in DigitalHeat terms, filler work is worth running whenever it earns more than the party's marginal cost, which is 0.226 CHF per server-hour for the chain and more under Deal A (see above). This gives the scheduler a clean rule for the July case.

**Meter on a submeter and credit the host's account.** Heata installs an accredited billing submeter, reads it remotely and credits the energy or bank account ([heata FAQ](https://www.heata.co/free-hot-water)). This makes "operator pays electricity" workable without a second grid connection. *Inference (open question):* whether IWB allows a third-party credit or a separate meter inside a Basel building was not researched and should be checked before a pilot.

**Size to hot-water baseload first.** Heata's entire offer is built on hot water, which runs all year, and Leafcloud demands a minimum hot-water load before it will site a rack ([Leafcloud](https://www.leaf.cloud/leafsites/)). Nerdalize needed an outdoor heat expeller to cope with summer ([Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1636612/its-a-server-no-its-a-radiator.html)). *Inference:* the model's single 1 kW server per house already follows this rule (87% utilisation, 3,261 of its 7,629 hours in Apr–Sep [model]). A 5–20 kW rack, though, produces 96–384 kWh of heat a day at full power, against 12 kWh/day of hot water in the reference house. Under Leafcloud's 5–20% sizing rule, such racks belong in buildings with roughly 25–400 kW design heat load, meaning apartment blocks, care homes and pools, not single-family houses.

**Pick a unit size that carries per-site overhead.** Heata's 0.33 kW units and the comparators' move to larger systems show that install, connectivity and visits sink small units. Critics of Heata named truck-roll costs and "no economies of scale" ([Register forum](https://forums.theregister.com/forum/all/2023/01/12/heata_offers_free_hot_water/)). *Inference:* DigitalHeat's 300 CHF/yr opex and 3,000 CHF install are both unsourced assumptions in the model. Spread across a 10 kW rack rather than a 1 kW server, they shrink roughly tenfold per kWh, which may be the biggest single lever available.

**Price compute directly, not through a marketplace.** Heata publishes its own price list, and its RTX 4090 add-on of £0.89 per GPU-hour is more than 2.5 times the 0.347 USD Vast.ai interruptible median the model uses, even before converting pounds to dollars ([pricing docs](https://docs.heata.co/pricing-billing/)). *Inference:* selling direct moves Deal A from −1,778 to −1,067 CHF and the Split to −661 CHF [model], and a green or sovereign premium could move it further. Heata's near-zero receivables are a warning, though. A high list price is worthless without volume, and sold share is the variable that has never been proven.

**Treat grants as the pilot budget and publish measured results.** Grants funded Heata's 100-prototype pilot and supplied about a quarter of its outside money ([GtR](https://gtr.ukri.org/organisation/45FE7A8F-A252-4E52-BBBD-C774B4E8CA93)). Yet no measured fleet results were ever published, which leaves its savings and carbon claims as company footnotes. *Inference:* a DigitalHeat pilot that publishes metered kWh of heat, utilisation and sold hours would hold evidence no competitor has. That also matches the case's 25% jury weight on transparency ([case brief](../docs/case-brief.md)). Swiss grant instruments were not researched here.

### Where Switzerland breaks the Heata analogy

| Factor | Heata (UK) | DigitalHeat (reference house) | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electricity price | Savings modelled at 22p/kWh electric, 7.8p/kWh gas ([heata unit page](https://www.heata.co/free-hot-water/heata-unit)) | 0.3325 CHF/kWh, IWB H4 [model] | Higher power cost makes Deal A more expensive for the operator |
| What server heat displaces | Immersion heater (COP 1) or gas boiler | Heat pump, COP 2.5 ([case brief](../docs/case-brief.md)) | See paragraph below: Swiss heat recovers about a third of the power bill |
| Unit size | ~0.33 kW electrical, ~0.2 kW into the tank | 1 kW server in the model; 5–20 kW racks in the product ([case presentation](../docs/case-presentation.md)) | DigitalHeat needs large hot-water sinks, not single homes |
| Heat coupling | Clamp-on through the wall of a vented copper cylinder, no plumbing | Heat exchangers into storage, heating and hot water at 50–70 °C ([case presentation](../docs/case-presentation.md)) | DigitalHeat pays for plumbing; Heata's cheap install does not transfer |

*Inference on heat value.* About 67% of each kWh Heata pays for reaches the tank. Offsetting an immersion heater, that heat is worth about 0.67 × 22p ≈ 14.7p, or two thirds of the electricity cost. Offsetting gas, it is worth 0.67 × 7.8p ≈ 5.2p, about a quarter. In the reference house, 0.8 kWh of heat replaces 0.32 kWh of heat-pump electricity, so **server heat recovers only 32% of its electricity cost**. Switzerland therefore looks like Heata's weaker gas case, not its stronger immersion case, and the compute price has to carry roughly two thirds of the power bill on top of hardware.

*Inference on hardware and temperature.* Heata relies on vented cylinders, a declining format, and still has no unvented version. DigitalHeat's design does not depend on the cylinder type, but its 50–70 °C target sits at the top of what operators report: Leafcloud states 45–65 °C ([Leafcloud](https://www.leaf.cloud/leafsites/)), and only Empa's lab system aims for 70 °C ([Netzwoche/Empa](https://www.netzwoche.ch/news/2024-04-14/waerme-aus-dem-rechner)). Heata preheats the bottom of the tank and leaves the existing heater to hold legionella-safe temperatures. That avoids any need to reach drinking-water temperatures from server heat alone, and DigitalHeat should copy the arrangement. The case's 80% heat-recovery assumption is also above Heata's ~67% clamp-on figure. Liquid-cooled racks plausibly do better, but the 80% figure is an assumption, not a measurement.

## Conclusion

Heata shows that the household side of heat-from-compute is the easy part. A free install, a refunded power bill and free hot water is an offer hosts accept, and the submeter-credit mechanism makes it administratively simple. What has not been solved in nine years, by Heata or by Qarnot with ten times the money, is filling the servers with paid work at a price above electricity plus hardware. For DigitalHeat this changes the question the hackathon should answer. "Who pays the electricity?" has a settled market answer: the operator does. The real questions are which compute buyer pays at least about 0.62 CHF per sold server-hour for interruptible, Swiss-hosted, deadline-backed work, and how much a 5–20 kW rack in an apartment block cuts the per-kWh install and opex that sink 1 kW units.

The most useful thing a team can show is the split between the deal and the chain. Under any deal the chain's deficit (or surplus) is set by compute price, sold share and hardware life, while the deal only decides who absorbs it. A small heat fee is justified less as revenue than as an incentive fix: it lets the operator run low-price filler jobs that keep the tank warm, which is exactly the behaviour Heata wrote into its SLA.
